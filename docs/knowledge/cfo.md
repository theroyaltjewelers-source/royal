## Cash versus profit

Profit is earned when work is delivered and recognized; cash moves when money is received or paid. A profitable business can run out of cash when it pays for materials and labor before clients pay. Cash is the constraint that ends companies; profit is the measure that sustains them.

Gross margin and cash flow answer different questions. Gross margin is the profit on each sale after direct costs. Cash flow is the money that actually moves in and out over a period. A job can carry a high gross margin and still drain cash when materials and labor are paid before the client pays the balance.

## Working capital

Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. Operating working capital is mainly receivables plus inventory minus payables. Deposits taken before work begins are a source of cash (a liability until earned). The cash conversion cycle is days inventory plus days receivable minus days payable: shorter is better.

## Accounts receivable and cash

A/R is money clients owe for work billed or delivered. Every dollar in A/R is cash not yet in the bank. Aging (current, 30, 60, 90+ days) shows collection risk; the older a balance, the less likely it is collected in full. Collecting balances before release, and taking deposits by stage, protects cash.

## Margins

Gross margin is revenue minus direct costs (materials, stones, direct labor, outside casting and setting), divided by revenue. Contribution margin also subtracts other variable costs (card fees, shipping). Operating margin subtracts overhead (rent, salaries, software, marketing). Margin is a percentage of price; markup is a percentage of cost: a 50 percent markup is a 33 percent margin.

## Break-even

Break-even revenue is fixed costs divided by contribution margin ratio. It tells you how much must be sold to cover overhead before earning profit.

## Forecasting and budgeting

A cash forecast lists expected receipts and payments by week for the next 13 weeks; it shows when cash runs short in time to act. A budget sets planned revenue and spending; variance analysis compares actual to plan and explains the difference (price, volume, mix, timing).

## Project profitability

Cost each commission: materials at actual cost, stones, labor hours at a loaded rate, outside services, and a share of overhead. Compare quoted margin with actual margin after delivery to find which kinds of jobs earn and which leak.

## Pricing economics

Price on value and on cost floors. A cost floor protects margin; value pricing captures what the work is worth to the client. Discounts fall straight off margin: a 10 percent discount on a 40 percent margin job removes a quarter of its profit. Metal prices move daily; quotes that hold metal prices for long periods carry commodity risk.

## Return measures

ROI is gain minus cost, divided by cost. ROAS is revenue attributed to advertising divided by ad spend; it ignores margin, so a ROAS that looks good can still lose money.

## Liquidity, debt and runway

Liquidity is cash available to meet obligations. Runway is cash divided by net monthly burn. Debt can fund growth or inventory but adds fixed payments; match its term to the asset it funds.

## Financial controls

Separate who approves, who pays and who reconciles. Reconcile bank and card accounts monthly. Require documentation for spending. Controls prevent loss and make numbers trustworthy.
