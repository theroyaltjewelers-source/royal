## Strategy

Strategy is a set of choices about where to play and how to win, and what not to do. A strategy is testable: it names the customer, the offer, the advantage and the trade-offs. Ambition without trade-offs is a wish, not a strategy.

## Competitive advantage

An advantage is durable only if competitors cannot copy it quickly (a durable advantage is often called a moat): brand and reputation built over years, proprietary relationships (stone dealers, setters, high-value clients), process know-how, and switching costs. For a bespoke luxury house the strongest advantages are trust, craftsmanship and a client experience others cannot imitate at the same quality.

## Capital allocation

Every dollar and every hour has alternative uses. Rank uses by risk-adjusted return and strategic value: protect cash needed to deliver committed work first, then fund the highest-return growth, then options. Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative given up; a profitable job can still be a bad decision if it displaces a better one or strains capacity.

## Prioritization

Prioritize by impact, urgency and reversibility. Irreversible, high-impact decisions deserve deliberate analysis; reversible, low-impact ones should be made fast and delegated. Limit work in progress: too many simultaneous priorities slows everything.

## Decision making

Separate facts, assumptions and judgment. Write down the decision, the options, the evidence, what would change your mind, and who decides. Pre-mortems (imagine the decision failed: why?) expose hidden risk. Decide with the information you have when waiting costs more than being wrong.

## Unit economics

Know what one unit of the business earns: for a commission, revenue minus materials, labor, outside services, and an allocation of overhead, and how long cash is tied up. Growth with negative or thin unit economics multiplies losses.

## Organizational design and delegation

Structure follows strategy: give each outcome one owner, clear decision rights and the information to act. Delegate outcomes with boundaries, not tasks. Escalation rules say what comes back to the founder.

## Operating cadence

A business runs on rhythm: daily triage of exceptions, a weekly review of pipeline, production, cash and commitments, monthly financials, quarterly priorities. Cadence turns strategy into execution.

## Risk and scenario planning

Identify concentration risks (one client, one supplier, one person), cash risks, and reputational risks. Plan base, downside and upside scenarios with trigger points that say when to act.

## Governance

Governance is who may decide what, with what checks. Even a founder-led company benefits from written authority limits, separation of duties for money, and records of major decisions.
